Context

Chapter Roadmap

Movement science research is saturated with designs where the same participants are measured more than once:

  • Pain scores at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks post-rehab
  • VO₂max before, midway, and after a 12-week conditioning program
  • Movement error across five practice blocks
  • Strength at pre, mid, and post-training

In all of these, the question centers on change within individuals over time. One-way repeated measures ANOVA is the natural tool.

This chapter covers

  • Why repeated measures designs are more powerful
  • Sphericity — the assumption unique to this design
  • GG and HF corrections when sphericity is violated
  • Bonferroni post hoc comparisons
  • Effect sizes: η²p, ω²p, Cohen's d
  • APA reporting for repeated measures
Running example

Muscular strength (kg) measured at pre, mid (6-wk), and post (12-wk) in 30 university students across a resistance training program.

Procedure

Workflow for One-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA

1Restructure data — wide format in SPSS: one row per participant, one column per time point
2Check assumptions — normality of difference scores and sphericity
3Run the repeated measures ANOVA and inspect Mauchly's test before reading the F-ratio
4Select the appropriate F-row — Sphericity Assumed, Greenhouse-Geisser, or Huynh-Feldt — based on Mauchly's result and epsilon
5If F is significant — run Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons
6Calculate effect sizes — η²p from SPSS output; ω²p from source table values; Cohen's d for each pairwise contrast
7Report in APA format — Mauchly's test, correction applied, omnibus F, post hoc results, and effect sizes

Logic

Within-Subjects vs. Between-Subjects Designs

Between-subjects ANOVA

  • Each participant in one group only
  • Individual differences (genetics, baseline fitness, motivation) stay in the error term
  • Error = group variability + individual differences
  • Large denominator → smaller F → lower power
The problem

In kinesiology, between-person baseline variability is enormous — it dominates the error term and masks real effects.

Within-subjects (repeated measures)

  • Each participant measured at every time point
  • Consistent individual baseline is mathematically separated out
  • Error = within-person inconsistency only
  • Small denominator → larger F → higher power
The advantage

Each person serves as their own control. The question becomes: relative to each person's own baseline, how much did they change?

06 / 22
Statistical Power

The Power Advantage: Removing Individual Baselines

Consider measuring grip strength at pre, mid, and post in a training program. Participant A (competitive athlete) starts at 110 kg; Participant B (sedentary) starts at 60 kg.

A between-subjects design treats that 50 kg difference as error — it inflates the denominator of F and obscures the training effect.

A repeated measures design asks instead: "Relative to each person's own starting point, how much did they change?" The 50 kg baseline difference cancels out entirely.

The correlation matters

The higher the correlation among repeated measurements, the more individual-difference variance is removed, and the greater the power advantage over a between-subjects design.

Sample size comparison

Medium effect, α = .05, power = .80, 3 time points, r = .50

Designn Required
Between-subjects≈ 52
Repeated measures≈ 28
Savings~46%

Fewer participants, same statistical power — a major practical advantage in clinical and sport science research.

🎯 Knowledge Check

A sport scientist says: "My repeated measures ANOVA needed only 28 participants to detect the same effect that a between-subjects ANOVA would need 52 for." What is the most accurate explanation?

B is correct. In a repeated measures design, some participants consistently score high and others consistently low across all time points. Because the same people appear at every measurement, that stable individual baseline is partitioned out of the error term entirely. What remains is only how each person's change pattern deviates from the group average — typically much smaller than raw between-person differences. A smaller error term produces a larger F for the same effect, which directly means fewer participants are needed to achieve the same power.

Statistics

How Variance Is Partitioned

SStotal = SSbetween subjects + SStime + SSerror

SSbetween subjects

Consistent individual differences in overall performance level — some participants score high across all time points, others consistently score low. Removed from the F-ratio entirely. Never appears in the denominator.

SStime

How much the group means change across the time points. This is the effect we test — it becomes the numerator of F.

SSerror (subjects × time)

How differently each participant responds across time points — individual inconsistency in change patterns. This is the denominator of F. Much smaller than between-subjects error.

Why this matters

In between-subjects ANOVA: F = MSgroups / MSwithin (includes all individual differences). In repeated measures: F = MStime / MSerror (individual differences excluded). Smaller denominator → larger F.

🎯 Knowledge Check

In a repeated measures ANOVA, SPSS output shows SSbetween subjects = 13,120, SStime = 444, SSerror = 111. What role does SSbetween subjects play in the F-ratio for the time effect?

C is correct. The key advantage of the repeated measures design is that SSbetween subjects — capturing consistent individual differences in overall performance level — is partitioned out as its own component and excluded from both numerator and denominator of the F-ratio for time. The F for time is MStime / MSerror, where MSerror reflects only within-person inconsistency across time points. The enormous SSbetween subjects simply drops out of the equation, which is precisely why the repeated measures F is so much larger than it would be in a between-subjects analysis of the same data.

Application

When to Use One-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA

Use it when:

  • The same participants provide data at 3 or more time points or conditions
  • The outcome variable is continuous
  • Every participant contributes one observation per time point
  • The goal is to test whether the population mean differs across time
Missing data

A participant with a missing time point cannot contribute to the full within-subjects comparison. Address missing data before analysis.

Connection to simpler tests

  • 2 time points only → reduces exactly to the paired t-test (identical results)
  • 2 levels → sphericity cannot be violated; Mauchly's test absent from SPSS output
  • 3+ time points → ANOVA required; pairwise t-tests would inflate familywise error rate
Nonparametric alternative

If the outcome is ordinal or normality is severely violated with small samples, use Friedman's ANOVA (Chapter 19).

09 / 22
Inference

Hypotheses and the Omnibus Test

Null hypothesis (H₀)

μpre = μmid = μpost

The population mean is equal at all time points — time has no effect on the dependent variable.

Alternative hypothesis (H₁)

At least one population mean differs from the others across time points.

The omnibus test as gatekeeper

A significant omnibus F only establishes that somewhere across the time points there is a meaningful change. It does not tell you which specific pairs differ. Post hoc Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons identify where the changes are.

Critical rule

Post hoc pairwise comparisons are only appropriate after a significant omnibus F. Running them after a non-significant result inflates Type I error.

α = .05 throughout — the familywise error rate is maintained at 5% by the omnibus test, avoiding the inflation that would result from multiple uncorrected t-tests.

Key Assumption

The Sphericity Assumption

Sphericity requires that the variances of the difference scores between all pairs of time points are approximately equal.

For a pre/mid/post design, sphericity requires:

Var(mid − pre) ≈ Var(post − pre) ≈ Var(post − mid)

In plain language: the consistency of each participant's change should be similar across all pairs of intervals.

Why it matters

When sphericity is violated, the standard F-test uses degrees of freedom that are too large — it rejects H₀ more often than the stated α. Type I error is inflated.

Common misconception

Sphericity is not about equal raw standard deviations at each time point. A dataset can have very different SDs at pre, mid, and post and still meet sphericity — what matters is whether the change scores are similarly variable across all pairs.

Violation example

Interval pre→mid: everyone improves 2 kg (very consistent → small variance)

Interval mid→post: some improve 8 kg, others plateau (very variable → large variance)

→ The two intervals have very different change variances → sphericity violated.

🎯 Knowledge Check

An exercise scientist tells a student: "Sphericity means the raw standard deviations at pre, mid, and post must be approximately equal." Is this correct?

C is correct. Sphericity is specifically about consistency of change, not consistency of raw spread. It asks whether the variability of (mid−pre) scores is similar to the variability of (post−pre) and (post−mid) scores. A dataset can have quite different raw SDs at pre, mid, and post and still meet sphericity — what matters is whether individual change scores are similarly variable across all pairs of time intervals. Treating sphericity as a raw-SD assumption is one of the most common misconceptions about this design.

Assumption Testing

Mauchly's Test of Sphericity

SPSS automatically produces Mauchly's W for every repeated measures ANOVA. W ranges from 0 (complete non-sphericity) to 1 (perfect sphericity).

Decision rule

  • p > .05 → sphericity not rejected; use Sphericity Assumed row
  • p < .05 → sphericity violated; apply GG or HF correction

Epsilon (ε) estimates

SPSS reports two epsilon estimates quantifying departure from sphericity. Epsilon ranges from 1/(k−1) to 1.0 (k = number of time points). For 3 time points, minimum ε = .50.

  • ε close to 1.0 → sphericity nearly met
  • ε far below 1.0 → serious violation
Sample size sensitivity

Mauchly's test is sensitive to sample size. With small samples, it may miss real violations. With large samples, trivial departures produce significant results. Always inspect the epsilon value alongside the p-value.

Practical guide

Mauchly's pεGGAction
> .05—Sphericity Assumed
< .05< .75Greenhouse-Geisser
< .05≥ .75Huynh-Feldt

Also apply GG/HF if ε is notably below 1.0 even when p is marginal, especially with small samples.

🎯 Knowledge Check

A researcher runs a repeated measures ANOVA on three time points and gets: Mauchly's W = .623, p = .001, εGG = .726, εHF = .789. What is the correct course of action?

B is correct. Because Mauchly's test is significant (p = .001), sphericity is violated and the uncorrected F-test cannot be used. The decision between GG and HF depends on the GG epsilon: when εGG < .75, Greenhouse-Geisser is the appropriate choice because the violation is substantial enough to warrant the more conservative correction. With εGG = .726 — just below the .75 threshold — GG applies here. Had εGG been .75 or above, Huynh-Feldt would be preferred as it corrects less aggressively and preserves more power when the violation is mild.

Corrections

Greenhouse-Geisser vs. Huynh-Feldt

Greenhouse-Geisser (GG)

  • Multiplies both df by εGG — more conservative
  • Use when εGG < .75 (serious violation)
  • Tends to overcorrect when violation is mild
  • F-statistic does not change — only df and p change

Huynh-Feldt (HF)

  • Uses a larger, less conservative epsilon estimate
  • Use when εGG ≥ .75 (mild violation)
  • Better balance of Type I error control and power
  • εHF is always ≥ εGG

The decision rule

p > .05Mauchly's not significant → use Sphericity Assumed
<.75p < .05 and εGG < .75 → use Greenhouse-Geisser
≥.75p < .05 and εGG ≥ .75 → use Huynh-Feldt
Always report the correction

Name the correction used and report the epsilon value. Readers need this to evaluate whether the p-value is trustworthy and to compare results across studies.

🎯 Knowledge Check

Two researchers analyze the same dataset. Researcher A applies Greenhouse-Geisser; Researcher B applies Huynh-Feldt. Both report the same F-statistic. Who will have the larger (less corrected) degrees of freedom?

C is correct. Both corrections work by multiplying the original degrees of freedom by an epsilon estimate. Because the Huynh-Feldt epsilon is always at least as large as the GG epsilon — and often considerably larger — HF applies less shrinkage to the degrees of freedom. Larger corrected df shifts the critical F value downward, generally resulting in a lower p-value. This is why HF is the less conservative correction and is preferred when εGG ≥ .75: the sphericity violation is mild enough that less aggressive correction still provides adequate Type I error control while sacrificing less power.

Follow-Up

Bonferroni-Corrected Pairwise Comparisons

With three time points (pre, mid, post), three pairwise comparisons are possible. SPSS produces these automatically via Estimated Marginal Means → Compare main effects → Bonferroni.

ComparisonMean Diff (kg)SEp (adj)95% CI
Mid − Pre2.02.27<.001[1.34, 2.70]

Post − Mid3.36.45<.001[2.22, 4.50]

All three comparisons significant — strength increased significantly at each stage of the program.

What to report for each comparison

  • Mean difference and its direction
  • Standard error (SE)
  • Bonferroni-adjusted p-value
  • 95% confidence interval
The gatekeeper rule

Post hoc pairwise comparisons are only appropriate after a significant omnibus F-test. Running them following a non-significant result capitalizes on chance and inflates Type I error — even with Bonferroni corrections applied.

16 / 22
🎯 Knowledge Check

A repeated measures ANOVA yields F(1.45, 42.11) = 1.93, p = .158, η²p = .06. A colleague suggests: "Just run the Bonferroni comparisons anyway — maybe pre vs. post is significant." What is wrong with this approach?

C is correct. The omnibus F-test acts as a gatekeeper: it must be significant before pairwise comparisons are warranted. Running pairwise tests after a non-significant overall result — hoping to find significance in individual pairs — is a form of fishing that inflates the probability of a false positive beyond the stated α level. Even with Bonferroni corrections applied, this practice is not defensible because the corrections assume the omnibus test was significant. A non-significant F tells you the data are consistent with no time effect; the appropriate response is to interpret that finding, not to search for any significant pair.

Effect Sizes

Partial Eta-Squared (η²p)

SPSS reports partial eta-squared as the default effect size. Unlike full η², the partial version excludes between-subjects variance from the denominator:

η²p = SStime / (SStime + SSerror)

Note: SSbetween subjects does not appear in this formula.

Cohen's benchmarks

η²pInterpretation
.01Small effect
.06Medium effect
.14Large effect
Why η²p is larger than full η²

By excluding SSbetween subjects from the denominator, η²p answers "what fraction of within-subject variance does time explain?" — not "what fraction of total variance?" In human performance data, between-subjects variance is often enormous, so η²p can be considerably larger than full η² from the same data. Always label it as partial.

Strength training example

η²p = 443.73 / (443.73 + 110.94) = .80

80% of within-subject (error + time) variance is accounted for by the time effect — a very large, consistent training effect across all 30 participants.

🎯 Knowledge Check

SPSS reports η²p = .80 for a repeated measures time effect. A classmate says "time explained 80% of the total variance in strength scores." What is wrong with this interpretation?

C is correct. Partial eta-squared is computed as SStime / (SStime + SSerror), deliberately leaving SSbetween subjects out of the denominator. In human performance research, between-subjects variance — individual differences in baseline ability — is often enormous. By excluding it, η²p answers "what fraction of within-subject variance does time explain?" rather than "what fraction of total variance?" The denominator is therefore much smaller than total SS, which makes η²p larger than full η² computed from the same data. Reporting it as "proportion of total variance" overstates the time effect's footprint in the overall data.

Effect Sizes

Partial Omega-Squared and Cohen's d

Partial omega-squared (ω²p)

Less biased than η²p — recommended for small-to-moderate samples. SPSS does not compute it; calculate from source table values:

ω²p = [(k−1)(MStime − MSerror)] / [(k−1)·MStime + (n−k+1)·MSerror]

k = number of time points, n = number of participants

Example: ω²p = [(2)(305.60 − 2.63)] / [(2)(305.60) + (29)(2.63)] = 605.94 / 687.47 ≈ .88

Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons

For each Bonferroni comparison, d is computed from the mean difference divided by the SD of the difference scores:

d = D̄ / SDD
ComparisondSize
Mid vs. Pre1.38Large
Post vs. Pre2.97Very large
Post vs. Mid1.36Large
Why d values are large here

Consistent training effects produce tight distributions of difference scores — small SDD in the denominator yields a large d, even for moderate mean differences.

18 / 22
🎯 Knowledge Check

In a between-subjects ANOVA, the error term includes individual differences among participants. What does MSerror contain in a repeated measures ANOVA instead?

C is correct. In a repeated measures design, consistent between-subjects variability — the fact that some people are consistently stronger or faster than others — is partitioned out as SSbetween subjects. The error term that remains (sometimes labeled subjects × time interaction or MSresidual) reflects how unpredictably each participant's score changes from one time point to another relative to the group trajectory. If everyone improved by exactly the same amount at each interval, this term would approach zero. What inflates it is inconsistency: some participants plateau while others surge. This residual inconsistency — not raw individual differences — is what the F-ratio is divided by.

Visualizing Repeated Measures Results

Line plot with 95% CIs

Shows means and precision at each time point. Use when communicating group-level change.

Spaghetti plot — individual trajectories

Each line = one participant. Lines mostly upward = small within-subject error, large F, high power. Crossing lines inflate MSerror.

19 / 22
🎯 Knowledge Check

A spaghetti plot shows that most individual lines move upward from pre to post, but about one-third of lines cross or reverse direction. Compared to a plot where all lines move cleanly upward, what does this crossing pattern suggest?

B is correct. The error term in a repeated measures ANOVA captures exactly how inconsistently participants change across time points. When spaghetti lines all move in the same direction, individual trajectories are similar — within-subject error is small, the F-ratio is large, and power is high. When lines cross or reverse, different participants are responding differently: some improve, others plateau, some decline. This inconsistency increases MSerror, which is the denominator of the F-ratio. A larger denominator produces a smaller F and reduces the probability of detecting the time effect as significant — even when the average trajectory is clearly upward.

Communication

APA Reporting Template and Example

Template

"A one-way repeated measures ANOVA examined the effect of [factor] on [DV]. Mauchly's test indicated that the sphericity assumption [was/was not] violated, W([df]) = [W], p = [p]. [Degrees of freedom were corrected using [GG/HF], ε = [ε].] The within-subjects effect was [significant/not significant], F([dftime], [dferror]) = [F], p = [p], η²p = [value]. Post hoc Bonferroni comparisons revealed that [pairwise results]."

Required elements
  • Mauchly's W, χ², df, p
  • Correction name + epsilon (if violated)
  • F with corrected df, exact p, η²p
  • Descriptives (M, SD) at each time point
  • Each pairwise: mean diff, SE, adj. p, 95% CI

Full example

"A one-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the effect of training time (pre, mid, post) on muscular strength. Mauchly's test indicated that the sphericity assumption was violated, W = .623, χ²(2) = 13.234, p = .001. Because εGG = .726, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The within-subjects effect of time was statistically significant, F(1.45, 42.11) = 116.0, p < .001, η²p = .80, ω²p = .88. Post hoc Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons indicated that strength increased significantly from pre-training (M = 79.7, SD = 12.3 kg) to mid-training (M = 81.7, SD = 12.3 kg), mean difference = 2.02 kg, p < .001, 95% CI [1.34, 2.70]; from mid- to post-training (M = 85.1, SD = 12.5 kg), mean difference = 3.36 kg, p < .001, 95% CI [2.22, 4.50]; and from pre- to post-training, mean difference = 5.38 kg, p < .001, 95% CI [4.54, 6.22]."

🎯 Knowledge Check

A student writes: "The within-subjects effect of time was significant, F(2, 58) = 116.0, p < .001, η²p = .80." Their professor says this report is incomplete. What critical element is missing?

B is correct. A complete APA report of a repeated measures ANOVA requires more than the F, df, p, and effect size. Because sphericity is a key assumption unique to within-subjects designs — and because violating it without correction inflates Type I error — readers need to know whether Mauchly's test was run, what it found, and which row of the SPSS output was used. The uncorrected df(2, 58) in this student's report is itself a red flag: sphericity correction typically produces fractional df like F(1.45, 42.11). Without reporting Mauchly's result and the correction applied, readers cannot evaluate whether the p-value is trustworthy.

Cautions

Common Pitfalls

1Ignoring Mauchly's test and always using "Sphericity Assumed." When Mauchly's p < .05 and epsilon is notably below 1.0, the uncorrected F-test rejects H₀ too often. Always check Mauchly's first.
2Interpreting η²p as proportion of total variance. Partial eta-squared excludes between-subjects variance from the denominator, making it larger than full η² from the same data. Always label it as partial and interpret accordingly.
3Running post hoc comparisons after a non-significant omnibus F. The same gatekeeper rule applies as in between-subjects ANOVA — pairwise tests are only appropriate following a significant overall F.
4Confusing the repeated measures error term with between-subjects within-groups error. MSerror in repeated measures ANOVA is the subjects × time interaction — individual inconsistency in change — not total within-group variability. These are conceptually and numerically different.

Wrap-Up

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

  • Repeated measures designs remove between-subjects variability from the error term → greater power with fewer participants
  • SS partitions into three parts: SSbetween subjects (removed), SStime (effect), SSerror (denominator of F)
  • Sphericity requires equal variance of pairwise difference scores — unique to within-subjects designs
  • Mauchly's W tests sphericity; epsilon quantifies severity
  • Decision rule: εGG < .75 → GG; εGG ≥ .75 → HF
  • Post hoc Bonferroni comparisons identify which time points differ — only after significant F
  • η²p is partial (excludes SSbetween subjects); ω²p corrects upward bias; Cohen's d for pairwise
  • APA write-up must include: Mauchly's W, correction applied, ε, corrected df, descriptives, pairwise results
